# Core Philosophy


A great knowledge video is not:


"Here is a concept. Let me explain it."


A great knowledge video is:


"You have experienced something.

You felt confused.

Here is the hidden system behind it."


The audience is not learning information.


The audience is discovering something about themselves.


---


# Film First Principle


Before explaining any concept, imagine this as a live-action documentary or short film.


Ask:


"If this idea was a story happening to a person, what would we film?"


Do not begin by designing graphics.


Begin by designing moments.


The video must prioritize:


- scenes over diagrams
- characters over icons
- environments over isolated graphics
- emotional moments over information density
- visual storytelling over direct explanation


The viewer should first experience the problem emotionally, then understand the mechanism intellectually.


A metaphor is not a graphic.


A metaphor is a visual event.


Example:


Weak:

"Show dopamine increasing with a graph."


Strong:

"Show a person alone at night repeatedly checking their phone. The room slowly changes as time disappears. Only later reveal the invisible reward system controlling the behavior."

# First 3 Seconds: The Scroll Stop Moment


The first 3 seconds determine whether the viewer continues watching.


The opening must immediately create:


- curiosity
- emotional tension
- surprise
- a question in the viewer's mind


The viewer should instantly think:


"Wait... what is happening?"


The first frame states what the film is about. One sentence. Ordinary words. Fully readable immediately, held for a few seconds while a moment is already happening underneath it. A mystery that never names its subject loses the audience. The sentence names the lesson, not a chapter title.


Do not show:


- title cards
- logos
- a second explanation after the opening sentence
- slow establishing shots
- generic animations
- abstract graphics without context


Start in the middle of a compelling moment.


The first frame should already contain a visual mystery, contradiction, or emotional situation.


The opening shot should feel like the first scene of a movie trailer.

# What a Finished Film Taught


These are features of a film that actually carried a viewer from confusion to a mechanism. They are production rules. They do not replace the philosophy above.


A title is not an introduction. A short mood line is not a rule. A number is not an explanation until the viewer can see what it belongs to.


---


## State the situation before the mechanism


After the opening mystery, play the problem as a sequence of events. Do not jump from the name of the topic to the dilemma.


Give the sequence enough time to be followed. If the music's length is fixed, shorten a later scene. Do not compress the rules to protect a later mood.


The sequence, in order:


1. What is at stake, and what is hidden.

2. What the viewer does first.

3. What the other person, who knows more, is required to do.

4. What remains after that action.

5. What each choice literally does. Define the verb. "Switch" means nothing until the line says which object is left behind and which object is taken.

6. The question the rest of the film will answer. One sentence. It stays on screen.


The picture changes on each step. The object the viewer just chose is marked only after the choice. The other person's action happens while that sentence is on screen. The two remaining choices are named only after both still exist.


---


## A caption is alone


The previous line is gone when the next one appears. A caption may not point backward.


Do not use "it," "he," "that," "one," "they," or "the other" unless the same sentence, or the picture in that frame, names the thing.


Name:


- the person, by role

- the object

- the quantity, and what it is a quantity of

- both sides of a comparison


Bad:


"Not even close."


"He will always have one."


"That chance becomes the prize."


Good:


"The two results are not even close."


"The host always has a losing prize to reveal."


"Switch, and that two thirds wins the prize."


Keep captions inside 5 to 12 words. Spend the words on the noun. A short line that could refer to two things on screen is a failed line.


If a comparison is on screen as two columns, the caption names both columns. If a total changes, the caption states the new total, not "more," unless the picture is actually adding that many.


---


## Labels are earned


Do not print the film's final terms on objects during the setup.


An object is unlabeled while it is only an object.


It receives a role when the viewer learns the role: "your pick," "the other one."


It receives the choice's name only in the sentence that defines that choice.


The resolved object, the one already opened and no longer part of the decision, is dimmed. Attention stays on what is still live.


---


## The sentence and the picture share one noun


If the line says what is hidden, those things are visible and labeled in that frame, while their containers are still closed.


If the line says someone reveals a losing prize, that container is opening on that word, and the prize is readable.


If the line says a quantity moves, a token moves from the first noun to the second noun during that line. The token is not a decoration sitting on top of a diagram. Its position is the claim.


A frame check, every time a line is added:


Render the moment the line is fully visible. Confirm it fits on one line, does not cover the object, and a viewer who sees only that frame can point at what the sentence means.


---


## Define a metaphor on contact


A physical stand-in for an invisible idea — a share, a chip, a beam, a coin — is undefined until the first caption says what it is a unit of.


"One share" is not a sentence.


"One share of the prize" is.


Later lines may use the coined word. Any line that carries the idea repeats what the unit belongs to, because the definition caption is no longer on screen.


---


## Show a transfer as two pictures


A counterintuitive result needs both pictures, in this order. One of them is not enough.


First picture:


A quantity leaves whatever was eliminated and gathers on what remains. Group the pair first, with a bracket or a shared mark. When one of the pair is removed, the group mark shrinks onto the survivor and the token travels with it.


Second picture:


Show what that quantity was made of. The usual first outcome is the losing one. Taking the other option turns that same quantity into the winning outcome. One marker starts on the losing object and arrives on the winning object.


Do not stop at "the other one has more." The viewer needs to see that their first outcome and the remaining prize are inverses, and that the chance follows the trade.


---


## Feel it, count it, then explain it


Order of revelation:


1. The wrong feeling, stated as a full sentence the viewer recognizes.

2. The constraint the viewer forgot. The knowing person's action is not random, and the caption says so by naming the action.

3. A count the viewer can see. Two labeled results. Small enough to count.

4. The same picture, scaled up, with the same labels. The caption names the scale.

5. Only then, the mechanism.


The false belief is written out and then crossed out.


Bad:


A vague correction: "It forgot."


Good:


The claim on screen: "Two remain, so their chances are equal."


A strike through the false clause.


The next line names what that claim left out.


---


## Keep the inside readable


If the point of a shot is what is inside, the mark sits in the center of the object, clear of the handle, the hinge, and the label beneath it.


A panel that only swings partway hides the mark and covers the label. Either open it fully, or leave it closed and show the mark through it.


Resolved objects stay in the frame, dimmed, so the viewer still sees what was removed. They do not compete with the live choice.


---


## One physical world


One typeface for titles, captions, and labels.


A dark field. One accent, used as a material: light, metal, liquid, a token. Not as a glow pasted on everything.


Objects have weight. A container has a hinge and a handle. A token has an edge. A strike is a line drawn through words.


No stick figures. No infographic chrome. No motion that does not change the claim.


Allowed motion:


- an object opening

- a token traveling

- a bracket shrinking onto what inherits a quantity

- a strike drawing across a false claim

- a count filling in

- a push-in on the thing just named


---


## The last line repeats its noun


When two lines form a thought and the first fades before the second appears, the second line repeats the noun. "It" has nothing left to point at.


If the anchor word is still on screen, the redefining sentence uses that word again. The ending changes the word's meaning in the sentence itself, not by gesture.


---


## Before calling a film finished


For every caption, answer:


1. If the previous caption were deleted, would this line still have a subject?

2. Can the viewer point at that subject in this frame?

3. Are the words on the objects words this scene has already defined?

4. If a quantity changes hands, does a token move between the two nouns in the sentence?

5. Does the introduction end on the question the film is about to answer, after the choices have been defined as actions?


If any answer is no, rewrite the line or the frame. Do not add a second caption to explain the first.

# What Teaching One Lesson Taught


These rules come from a film that looked finished and still could not be followed. A viewer who cannot say what the film is about, cannot say what an analogy is for, or watches a picture that does not move, has not been taught. Style does not survive that failure.


The lesson is one sentence in ordinary words. Every world exists to move that sentence forward. A beautiful scene that could be cut into a different film does not belong in this one.


---


## Say the subject on the first frame


The first frame carries one sentence. It is fully opaque at frame 0. It stays a few seconds. It says what the film is about, in words a person who has never studied the topic would use.


"How a learner starts wrong, and how training makes it less wrong."


The picture under the sentence is already a moment: a light, a room, a first failure. The sentence is not a title card, a logo, or a chapter name. After it fades, the film shows the example. It does not explain the mechanism yet.


A silent mystery, a slow wake-up, or a mood line is not an introduction. If the first second does not name the lesson, the opening is unfinished.


---


## One example survives every world


Before the first metaphor, lock two outcomes. Name both. One is what the learner produces. One is what was asked for. They are specific: a word, a dish, a door, a number the viewer can point at.


From the moment both exist, both stay readable in the same corner of every frame, in the same words, until the wrong outcome becomes the right one.


TARGET quiet · GUESS salt


A new world that does not use those names is a different film. The chef, the friends, the mountain, and the network are the same pair in a new place. The first sentence of each world says so.


The corner line is not a caption. The caption still has to stand alone. The corner line is how the pair survives after the caption leaves.


---


## An analogy opens by saying its job


The first caption of a new world is a question or a purpose. It names what this picture is for in the lesson, using the two outcomes.


"How does a weight step when the valley is hidden?"


"Which weight made the guess salt? Look backward."


"This kitchen shows how the wrong guess gets built."


Then tell only what a person in that place can see and do. The technical name comes after that action is visible.


For a walk that stands in for a search:


1. Name the question this place answers.

2. Say where the person is, and what hides the rest.

3. Say the only information at their feet, and the verb that uses it. "Downhill" means the ground falling beneath their feet. They step that way, then feel again.

4. Show the step that fails, and say why that failure is a problem.

5. Show the step that stays on the slope.

6. Only then map the place: this mountain is every setting, height is the error, the person is the current setting, and repeating the walk is the method.


"The climber is one set of weights" before the viewer has seen a mountain, fog, and a step is a failed analogy. The mapping is the last caption of the place, not the first.


---


## A definition that names no trouble is unfinished


A teaching line has two parts. The first names the thing. The second says what trouble that thing causes, or what trouble it does not solve. The learner's situation is in the sentence: stuck, wrong, blocked, too far.


Bad:


"The loss says how wrong, not which weight."


Good:


"Stuck: the loss says how wrong, not which weight."


The next world opens by repeating that trouble as its reason for existing.


"Which weight made the guess salt? Look backward."


A definition that could sit in a glossary, with no one stuck because of it, does not earn the next scene.


---


## The picture moves, and the move is continuous


The framing changes when the claim changes. A push, a drift, a widening, a fade through black. The camera's next position begins where it already is and eases there. A snap to a new scale, a flash that replaces the picture, or a new world that appears already mid-sentence is a cut.


Between worlds, fade through black. Inside a world, do not cut. The subject can cross the frame. The scale can grow. The move takes long enough to follow.


A graphic that holds still for a whole caption is a slide. Motion that does not change what the sentence claims is decoration. The allowed moves are the ones already named: an opening, a token, a strike, a count, a push toward the noun just said, a widening when the system gets larger.


Four turns earn a visible hit, on the same frame as the sentence: the wrong outcome locking in, the measurement proving the learner is stuck, the step that goes too far, and the wrong outcome becoming the right one. The hit changes the object. It is not a cut to another movie.


---


## The label sits on its noun


A number is a weight only while it sits on the connection the sentence is talking about. A role sits on the person or the plate it names. A word floating in empty space above a diagram is not a label. If the caption says "under his feet," the mark is on the ground at the feet, in that frame.


---


## Before calling a teaching film finished


Answer these in addition to the caption checks above:


1. Can a viewer quote the lesson after the first second, before any metaphor?

2. Can they name the wrong outcome and the right outcome in the middle of every world?

3. Can they say, in one sentence, why the current world exists?

4. If the world is an analogy, can they describe the person's limit and action without using the technical term?

5. Does the latest definition say who is stuck, and does the next world start from that stuckness?

6. Did the camera glide or fade into this frame, rather than appear there?


If any answer is no, rewrite the opening line of that world or the move into it. Do not add a second caption to explain a line that never stated its job.

# Layperson Review Before Polish


This is the same kind of gate as a design review before building. A cut that looks finished still goes through it. Style, music lock, and a dark field do not exempt it.


<HARD-GATE>
Once a cut exists, do not add a caption, a metaphor, a scene, or code to make the film clearer until both reviews below are written, the action plan is written in the required shape, and the user has approved that plan. This applies when the user asks for a critical review, a polish, production grade, or "make it teach." It also applies when you are about to call the film finished.
</HARD-GATE>


## Who you are for this pass


You write as two people, in this order. You do not blend them into one generous reader.


1. A person who has never studied the topic. They know ordinary life. They do not know the field's words. If the film is about how a machine learns, they do not know weight, loss, backpropagation, or gradient descent. They only know what this cut just showed them. Forget what you meant while building it. If you cannot point at it in a frame, you did not learn it.

2. A filmmaker. You judge whether this is a film. The object in the sentence moves while the line is up. The camera eases. Worlds join by a fade through black. A zoom on a still drawing is not a film.


## Checklist


Complete these in order. Do not start the plan at step 5.


1. Watch the cut, or render the frame where each caption is fully visible. Do not review from memory of the storyboard.

2. Write what the person who knows nothing can actually repeat.

3. Write where the teaching breaks, one world at a time.

4. Write the one sentence the film must leave behind.

5. Write the action plan in the shape below.

6. Show the plan to the user. Stop. Do not implement it until they approve.


## What the review must contain


The review is not a score and not a list of taste notes. It is these parts, in this order.


### What a viewer who knows nothing actually takes away


One paragraph, in their words. Say what they could teach back after the film. If they could name the moves but could not repeat the action, say that. A generous summary that uses words the film never earned is a failed paragraph.


### Where the teaching breaks


One bullet per world. Name the scene. Quote the claim the caption makes. Then say what the picture does during that line. A caption on a picture that holds still is a break. A technical word that arrives before the action is a break. An analogy whose first line uses the term the analogy was supposed to teach is a break.


### The one idea the film must leave behind


One sentence in ordinary words. No second example. Keep the pair of outcomes already on screen. The jargon comes back only as names for moves the viewer has already watched.


### Action plan


Keep the worlds, the music lock, and the visual world. Change what happens inside them. The plan has four parts.


**One token.** Name the object that is the guess, the miss, or the thing at stake. It is born in the opening, and it is the same object in every world until the ending changes it. If a scene cannot show that object doing the verb in the caption, the caption is early.


**Purpose, then proof, then name.** Each world already opens with a purpose line. The next two seconds perform that purpose before any new term. For each world, write the purpose, the action the viewer will see, and only then the name that action earns.


**Motion without cuts.** Camera moves stay eased. Scene joins stay fades through black. The new motion is inside the shot: the token, the gap, the share, the step. Do not add flash cuts.


**Files and verbs.** One line per file. The verb is what the token does there: lands, grows, stands, splits, steps, becomes the right outcome.


### Check


For each world, name two frames: the frame where the purpose line is fully visible, and the frame where its verb finishes. A viewer who sees only that pair can point at the subject of the sentence. If they cannot, the line or the frame is wrong. Do not add a second caption to explain the first.


## Rationalizations


| Excuse | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "The pictures are already good." | A viewer who cannot teach the idea back was not taught. Write the takeaway first. |
| "I'll add a clearer caption." | A second caption does not move the object. The plan names the verb. |
| "I know what the scene means." | The person who has never studied the topic does not. Write only what the frame shows. |
| "The plan is obvious, I'll just fix it." | The plan is the review. Show it, then stop. |
| "This cut is too far along to review." | A finished-looking cut is exactly when this gate applies. |
